PayPal payment plans — walkthrough

Everything I would have covered on a call, written down so you can re-read it, forward it, and hold me to it.

You asked for PayPal subscriptions. Once you described what you actually wanted — set the number of months a client should pay the remaining balance, they choose 3, 4 or 6, then their account gets charged monthly — that turned out to be a different product. So there are two halves here, and the instalment half is the bigger one.

Payment plans — the main thing

Your staff enter what a customer owes and which lengths to offer. The customer opens a link, picks one, and PayPal takes it monthly until the balance is clear, then stops by itself.

Subscription plans — the second half

Ordinary recurring plans, monthly or yearly, at any rate you like, created from wp-admin without touching config files.

1. What your customer sees

One link per customer. No account, no login, nothing to install. The figures on the cards are the real amounts that will be charged — not "from $x".

2. Invoice first, deposit, then the plan

You said you invoice each customer separately, that some pay a deposit and carry a larger remaining balance, and that you want the plan set up after the invoice goes out. That is exactly the shape this takes. There is no price list anywhere in it — every customer is quoted on their own.

  1. Invoice them however you already do. Nothing here sends invoices, and nothing here needs to change about how you raise them.
  2. Take the deposit however you already take it. Cash, transfer, card, a PayPal payment — it does not matter, because that money never passes through this.
  3. Balance owed = the invoice total minus the deposit. This is the only figure PayPal ever charges.
  4. Deposit already paid = what you took, for the record. It is stored against the plan and shown on both screens, and it is never charged again.
  5. Reference = your invoice number, so their page and their invoice carry the same one.

So a $1,340 invoice with a $500 deposit is entered as balance 840.00, deposit 500.00. Here is what that customer opens:

The one mistake this form lets you make. Typing the invoice total into Balance owed while also filling in the deposit charges the customer for money they have already handed over. Nothing can detect that from the outside — both numbers are perfectly legitimate — so instead the confirmation reads the arithmetic back at you:

840.00 USD will be split into monthly payments — invoice 1340.00 USD less the 500.00 USD deposit already received.

Read that line before you send the link. It is the only guard there can be.

A deposit is not a first instalment. If you would rather PayPal collected the deposit as part of the plan, do not use that field — put the whole balance on the plan and let the first payment be the deposit.

Two things worth being straight about

Nothing here raises or sends the invoice. If you want PayPal to issue the invoice too — created and emailed from this same screen, then the plan opened for whatever is left — that is a real addition rather than a setting, and I would rather scope it properly than pretend it is already there. Say the word and I will.

"Withdraw from their account" needs one click from them, once. PayPal will not let anyone — me, you, or itself — start pulling money from a customer without that customer approving it first. They approve the plan once, and from then on PayPal takes each month automatically with nothing further from them. That first approval is not something I can remove, and you would not want a payments provider that let me.

6. The one decision I need from you

Should a longer plan cost more?

Right now it does not. $1,000 costs $1,000 whether the customer takes 3 months or 6, and the page says "no extra charge" on every option. That is a real business decision and I am not going to invent a markup on your behalf.

If you want longer terms priced higher — interest, an admin fee, whatever — tell me the numbers and it is a small change. My only strong opinion: it must be shown on the customer's screen, not buried in the arithmetic.

Why the odd cent moves

$1,000 over 3 months is 333.33 recurring. Somebody has to absorb the cent. It goes on the first payment — 333.34, then 333.33, then 333.33 — never the last. If it went last, a customer who cancels part-way through is left with a closing figure that matches nothing anyone quoted them.

7. What this does not do

  • It does not chase a debt. Cancelling a plan stops PayPal charging; the outstanding balance stays on record so a human can follow it up. Nothing here sends demand letters.
  • It does not change a plan mid-flight. PayPal freezes a plan's schedule once a customer approves it. Changing someone's balance means cancelling and issuing a new plan for the remainder.
  • It does not take cards directly. Everything goes through PayPal checkout. No card details ever touch your site, which is the point.
  • It is not a credit check. If a customer's card fails, PayPal retries and the plan is flagged payment_failed on your screen. Deciding what to do about that is yours.
  • It does not do partial refunds automatically. A refund issued in PayPal is recorded here and the paid-to-date recalculated, but the balance is not re-spread across the remaining months.
  • No design work is included. The customer page inherits your theme's fonts and colours rather than imposing its own.

8. How I have proved it works so far

Everything above was built before any PayPal credentials existed, so it has been verified the ways that do not need an account:

  • 212 automated checks, no network needed — 146 on the library, 66 on the WordPress side.
  • The screenshots on this page are the real screens, rendered from the actual plugin code against WordPress's own admin stylesheets. They are not mock-ups, so they cannot flatter the thing they show.
  • Mutation testing. I break the code on purpose and check the tests notice. One break survived: making the quote ignore the chosen term, so every option showed the 3-month price. All the tests still passed, because the only figure I had asserted was the total — which is identical across terms by design. That is exactly the bug a customer would have seen. Assertions rewritten; that break now fails four checks.
  • The deposit work above got the same treatment. I broke it six ways — the deposit dropped on save, added to the balance instead of subtracted from it, an unreadable figure silently rounded to zero, and so on. One of the six survived: nothing tested the path where staff actually type a deposit in, because every test read arrangements that already existed. Six more checks now drive the form itself, and all six breaks are caught.
  • Reading those screenshots caught three real faults that no test was looking for: a running plan that greeted the customer with "your payment plan is set up" on every visit for six months, a destructive Cancel button styled identically to a harmless one, and a settings screen dumping raw JSON at you.

The one that would have cost you money. PayPal lets you override a plan's prices per customer — that is what makes one reusable plan work for every balance. If PayPal ever ignores that override it does not return an error. It creates the subscription, hands back a perfectly good approval link, and then charges the template's placeholder price of 1.00 a month.

So the code reads the plan back off PayPal after creating it, compares every payment against what it calculated, and refuses to hand over the approval link if they differ. The customer sees an error instead of a plan. Nothing has been charged at that point.

9. The things you would have asked on a call

Why not just use a subscriptions plugin?

Because none of them do this. Off-the-shelf subscription plugins sell a fixed product at a fixed price forever. You need an arbitrary balance split over a term the customer picks, stopping on its own. Your brief also specifically asked for the REST API rather than a plugin, and that turned out to be the right call.

Where does the money go?

Straight into your PayPal account, on your PayPal app, under your own Developer credentials. Nothing routes through me. I never hold your credentials beyond the sandbox testing, and the live ones go into your wp-config.php, not into anything of mine.

How does the customer get their link?

You copy it off the detail screen and send it however you already talk to them — email, WhatsApp, on an invoice. Anyone holding the link can set that one plan up, so treat it exactly like an emailed invoice link. It exposes that single balance and nothing else: no other customer, no list, no account.

What if a payment fails?

PayPal retries on its own schedule. The plan shows as payment_failed on your screen and clears itself when a charge succeeds. If the retries run out and the plan ends with money still owed, it is flagged ended_short — the one status worth watching, because it means a human needs to make a phone call.

Can the customer pay it off early?

Not through this page as built. PayPal subscriptions bill on a schedule; there is no "settle the rest now" button in them. If you want that it is a separate one-off payment button that cancels the plan on success — a small addition, but an addition, so say the word and I will scope it rather than quietly assume it.

What happens if the plugin is removed?

Payment history stays. Uninstalling deliberately does not drop the two database tables, because a record of who paid you what should outlive a plugin folder. Subscriptions already running at PayPal also keep running — they live at PayPal, not on your site.

Can two people set up the same plan twice?

No. The term is checked server-side against what that specific customer was offered, so editing the request gets a 400 rather than a cheaper plan. Each attempt carries an idempotency key, repeat webhook deliveries are rejected by a unique index on PayPal's sale id, and paid-to-date is recalculated from the ledger rather than added to — so a replayed message cannot double-count.

Who owns the code?

You do. It is in a private repository and I will add you as a collaborator whenever you send me a GitHub username; the full source is also in the zip in our chat. No licence key, no phone-home, no dependency on me being around. It is plain PHP with no third-party libraries at all.

Why won't you get on a call?

Straight answer: I work text-only here on Freelancer — no phone, no video, no screen share. It is a hard limit on how I work, not a preference. If that is a dealbreaker for you I would rather know now than waste your time.
